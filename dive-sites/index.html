Hawaiʻi Dive Sites

27 sites on four islands, each briefed with depth, entry, training, protected-area status and season. Start here to match the right coast to your dates.

Best Diving Hawaiʻi · Updated October 2026 · How we research

Where is the best diving in Hawaiʻi?

It depends on the season. The Kona coast of Hawaiʻi Island is sheltered and diveable nearly year-round, with the manta night dive. Maui's Molokini and the Lānaʻi Cathedrals are the classic boat dives, Oʻahu has the best wrecks, and Kauaʻi has caverns. North shores open in summer; south shores are calmest in winter.

There is no single best dive site in Hawaiʻi, and anyone who tells you otherwise has not dived here in more than one season. The islands sit in the open Pacific with no continental shelf to soften incoming swell, so where the diving is good depends on the month, the direction the swell is coming from and the wind. A site that is flat and gin-clear in July can be a white-water hazard in January.

This hub is built to help you match place to time. Every major site we cover is briefed on its island page with the same set of facts: the published depth range, how you get in, the training a shop will normally expect, whether it is inside a Marine Life Conservation District (MLCD), where to park or which harbor the boats use, and which county ocean-safety agency covers it. Each card also carries a month-by-month strip showing how its exposure usually plays out.

Pick by What You Want

Your first dives, or your first since certification

  • Molokini Crater (inside): Reef profiles usually 20–70 ft; the cove floor slopes to about 100 ft before dropping off, boat, discover scuba (with instructor) and up.
  • Ulua Beach: About 10 ft at the beach to 40 ft or more on the outer reef, shore, discover scuba (with instructor) and up.
  • Koloa Landing: Mostly 10–40 ft, about 50 ft at the deepest, shore, discover scuba (with instructor) and up.

All three have calm, shallow sections, lots of fish and instructors who use them every week. Hanauma Bay on Oʻahu is a superb place to snorkel but is not where most new divers train.

Wrecks

  • YO-257 & San Pedro wrecks: 60–100 ft, boat, advanced open water recommended.
  • Sea Tiger: Deck around 90 ft, sand deeper, boat, advanced open water recommended.
  • Corsair plane wreck: Roughly 100–115 ft on open sand, boat, advanced open water recommended.
  • Mahi shipwreck: About 70–90 ft, boat, advanced open water recommended.

Oʻahu has the state's best wreck diving, but most of it sits deeper than the 60-foot Open Water limit. Plan on Advanced certification or a guided profile.

Shore diving

  • Shark's Cove & Three Tables: About 10–45 ft, shore, open water.
  • Five Caves / Mākena Landing: About 30–40 ft, shore, open water.
  • Two Step (Hōnaunau Bay): Shallow along the shore; reef slopes to about 80 ft over sand mid-bay, shore, open water.
  • Puakō: Mostly 15–35 ft, shore, open water.
  • Koloa Landing: Mostly 10–40 ft, about 50 ft at the deepest, shore, discover scuba (with instructor) and up.

Our shore diving guide covers lava entries, dive flags and the state rules that apply.

Big animals

  • Manta Village (Keauhou): About 25–40 ft on sand and rubble, boat, open water.
  • Garden Eel Cove (Manta Heaven): About 30–45 ft, boat, open water.
  • Molokini Back Wall: Drift profiles about 40–100 ft along a face that drops past 200 ft, boat, advanced open water recommended.
  • Niʻihau & Lehua: Wall and arch profiles; operators set depths per site, boat, advanced open water recommended.

Turtles are close to guaranteed almost everywhere. Mantas at night off Kona are as close to reliable as wildlife gets, though never certain. Humpback whales are heard far more often than seen; federal rules keep everyone 100 yards away.

Caverns and lava tubes

  • First & Second Cathedral, Lānaʻi: First Cathedral about 25–60 ft; Second Cathedral about 15–50 ft, boat, open water.
  • Sheraton Caverns: About 30–60 ft, boat, open water.
  • Shark's Cove & Three Tables: About 10–45 ft, shore, open water.
  • Tunnels (Makua) Beach: About 15–60 ft along the outer reef, shore, open water.

These are daylight caverns, not cave dives, but surge inside lava tubes can be strong. Skip them on swell days.

Season by Swell Direction

The single most useful planning idea for Hawaiʻi is that swell is seasonal and directional. University of Hawaiʻi surf climatology describes large northwest swells reaching north and west shores in winter and smaller south swells, often 3 to 9 feet, reaching south shores in summer after a week-long trip from Southern Hemisphere storms. Trade winds blow from the northeast most of the year. Hawaiʻi Island's Kona coast sits in the lee of three large volcanoes and is sheltered from much of it.

These strips are tendencies, not forecasts. A winter south swell or a summer north swell can arrive at any time. The National Weather Service in Honolulu issues high surf advisories by shore: 15 feet for north-facing shores, 12 feet for west-facing shores (8 feet on the Big Island), and 10 feet for south- and east-facing shores. If your planned coast is under an advisory, choose another coast. For month-by-month water temperature and visibility, see the best time to dive Hawaiʻi; for a filtered list for your dates, use the Dive Site Finder.

Certification Levels in Plain Terms

  • Discover Scuba / try dives. No certification; you dive with an instructor in shallow water, usually no deeper than 40 feet. Molokini's inside reef, Ulua Beach and Koloa Landing are typical venues.
  • Open Water. Certified to about 60 feet. Most shore dives, the manta night dive, the Lānaʻi Cathedrals and Sheraton Caverns are within range.
  • Advanced Open Water. Certified to about 100 feet. Needed or strongly recommended for the Oʻahu wrecks, Molokini's Back Wall, Brennecke's Ledge and Niʻihau.

Shops can and do ask for logbooks and recent experience, especially for night, drift and deep dives. If you have not dived in a year or more, book a refresher before the trip. Our certification guide explains the options.

Protected Areas You Will Dive In

Hawaiʻi's Marine Life Conservation Districts are no-take reserves managed by the state Division of Aquatic Resources. Hanauma Bay became the first in 1967. The districts divers visit most are Hanauma Bay, Pūpūkea and Waikīkī on Oʻahu; Molokini Shoal, Honolua–Mokulēʻia and Mānele–Hulopoʻe in Maui County; and Kealakekua Bay, Old Kona Airport, Waiʻaleʻa Bay and Lapakahi on Hawaiʻi Island. Kauaʻi has none. Inside them you may not take or harm fish, shells, coral, rock or other marine life, and some have extra rules on fish feeding, anchoring or commercial permits. Read the MLCD rules guide before you go.

Who to Check With Before You Dive

Each county runs its own lifeguard service: Honolulu Ocean Safety on Oʻahu, the Maui County Ocean Safety Bureau, Hawaiʻi Fire Department Ocean Safety on the Big Island and the Kauaʻi Ocean Safety Bureau. Towers are at the busier beaches; most dive entries are unguarded. The statewide Hawaii Beach Safety site shows posted hazards at guarded beaches, and the state Department of Health posts brown-water advisories after heavy rain. Our conditions and safety guide explains how to read all of it.

Hawaiʻi has one emergency recompression facility, in Honolulu. Divers injured on Maui, the Big Island or Kauaʻi are flown to Oʻahu, so conservative profiles and dive accident insurance matter more here than in many destinations.

Planning a Two-Island Dive Trip

Plenty of divers split a week between two islands, and the order matters more than people expect. Inter-island flights are short, but they still count as flying after diving, so the island you dive first should be the one you leave first, with a dive-free morning before the hop. A common pattern is to start on Oʻahu or Maui, fly to Kona mid-trip on a non-dive day, and finish with the manta night dive and Kona boat days, leaving a full day free before the flight home.

Pair islands by season. In winter, Kona and the south-facing coasts of Maui and Oʻahu make a strong combination, because both are sheltered from the North Pacific. In summer, add a north-shore island: Oʻahu's Pūpūkea or Kauaʻi's Tunnels and the Niʻihau charters. Whatever you choose, keep a weather day in reserve on each island. Boats cancel for wind and swell, and the trips most likely to be cancelled, Niʻihau and Molokini's Back Wall, are also the ones people most regret missing.

Finally, if a volcano summit is on the list, do it on arrival day or the day after, before your first dive on that island. That single scheduling choice removes Hawaiʻi's most common altitude mistake.

Altitude: Hawaiʻi's Hidden Dive Hazard

Few dive destinations put a 10,000-foot summit within a two-hour drive of the reef. Haleakalā on Maui, Saddle Road and Maunakea on the Big Island and, to a lesser degree, Kōkeʻe on Kauaʻi are all ascents to altitude after diving. Divers Alert Network treats ground ascents like flights. Our flying and altitude guide lists the elevations and the waiting times.

Choose the coast for the season, the site for your training, and the day for the swell. The rest is just getting wet.
