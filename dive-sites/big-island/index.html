Big Island Dive Sites

Resident mantas at night, lava tubes and coral by day, and the most sheltered coast in Hawaiʻi. Every major Kona and Kohala site with its depth, entry, season and rules.

What is the best diving on the Big Island?

The Kona coast is Hawaiʻi's most reliable dive coast, sheltered by the volcanoes and diveable on most days of the year. Do the manta night dive (about 25–45 ft at Manta Village or Garden Eel Cove), Kealakekua Bay's Kaʻawaloa Cove (5–120 ft, an MLCD), and shore dives at Two Step and Puakō. Avoid Saddle Road and Maunakea after diving.

Hawaiʻi Island has the most reliable diving in the state, and the reason is geography. Its west coast sits in the lee of Mauna Loa, Mauna Kea and Hualālai, which block the trade winds, and the young lava coastline drops off quickly into deep, clear water. On most days of the year, somewhere along the Kona and Kohala coasts is calm.

The island is also where Hawaiʻi's most famous dive happens: the night manta dive off Kona, where resident reef mantas feed on plankton drawn to divers' lights. It deserves its reputation. But Kona also has excellent daytime lava diving, the protected coral of Kealakekua Bay and some of the best shore diving in the state, and it is worth staying long enough to do more than one night.

The Kona and Kohala Coasts

  • North Kona (airport to Kailua). Honokōhau Harbor is the main dive-boat base. Garden Eel Cove, the second big manta site, is just north near the airport. Old Kona Airport's protected reef is on the edge of town.
  • South of Kailua (Keauhou to Hōnaunau). Manta Village off Keauhou, then the Kealakekua Bay MLCD and the shore entry at Two Step beside Puʻuhonua o Hōnaunau.
  • South Kohala (Waikoloa to Puakō and beyond). Long stretches of lava reef with chimneys and tunnels. Puakō is the classic shore dive. This coast is a little more exposed to big winter northwest swells.

The east (Hilo) side is wet and windward and has little recreational diving infrastructure. Visiting divers base themselves in Kona.

Why Kona Dives Year-Round

Big Island boat operators run nearly every day of the year, which is unusual in Hawaiʻi. The volcanoes take the trades off the coast, and winter's large North Pacific swells mostly pass by the Kona coast or arrive much reduced. That does not mean Kona never closes. The National Weather Service sets a lower high-surf threshold for west-facing shores of the Big Island than for the other islands (an advisory at 8 feet and a warning at 12 feet), because this coast's shoreline is steep and unforgiving when swell does arrive. Southerly kona storms and occasional big south or west swells are the other spoilers. The state notes that Kealakekua Bay becomes dangerous in high surf and southerly storms, even though its Kaʻawaloa corner is almost always calm.

Water temperature changes more than conditions do: low to mid-70s °F in late winter, around 80 °F in late summer. On the manta dive you spend 45 minutes kneeling or hovering in the dark at night, which feels colder than a daytime reef dive at the same temperature. A 5 mm suit or a hood is not overkill.

Manta Night Dive: The Essentials

The Kona mantas are reef mantas, a resident population that researchers have tracked for decades. Two sites carry most of the trips. Manta Village off Keauhou is usually the calmer, at roughly 25 to 40 feet. Garden Eel Cove near the airport, which operators call Manta Heaven, is a little deeper at about 30 to 45 feet and more exposed. Divers settle on the sand around a light source; snorkelers float above on lit boards. The lights attract plankton and the mantas come in to feed, often passing within a few feet.

The rules are simple: do not touch, do not chase, keep your light pointed up, stay low and let the mantas come to you. Since 2009, Hawaiʻi law (Act 92) has made it an offense to knowingly catch or kill a manta ray in state waters. Our manta dive briefing covers site choice, gear and what a typical evening looks like.

Shore Diving the Kona Coast

Kona's shore diving rewards planning. Entries are usually lava, not sand: Two Step has a natural ledge you can step off, Puakō has several rough access points along Puakō Beach Drive, and Old Kona Airport has a broad reef shelf. Watch several sets before entering, choose your exit before you go in, and leave if the surge is moving rocks. Local shore-diving guides suggest swell of no more than about 2 feet at Old Kona Airport and 4 feet at Mahukona. Read our shore diving guide for flags, entries and the state rules that apply.

Protected Waters and Wildlife Rules

Three MLCDs on this coast matter to divers: Kealakekua Bay, Old Kona Airport and Waiʻaleʻa Bay in Kohala. Kealakekua's rules prohibit taking or possessing marine life, shells, coral and rock and also prohibit fish feeding; Old Kona Airport's rules protect shells, sand and coral from being altered or taken. See the MLCD rules guide for the details.

Hawaiian spinner dolphins rest by day in Kona's bays, including Kealakekua and Hōnaunau. A federal rule that took effect in 2021 prohibits approaching within 50 yards of them, swimming with them or causing a vessel to approach them, within two nautical miles of the main islands. If dolphins arrive while you are in the water, carry on with your dive and let them pass. Turtles get the NOAA-recommended 10 feet.

Safety, Altitude and the Drive Home

Lifeguards on Hawaiʻi Island are part of the Hawaiʻi Fire Department's Ocean Safety section, which staffs towers at a limited set of beaches and in 2026 reported vacancies among its water safety officers. Almost no Kona dive entry has a tower. Check Hawaii Beach Safety and the NWS surf forecast before shore diving, dive with a buddy and leave a plan with someone ashore.

Altitude is the Big Island trap. Daniel K. Inouye Highway (Saddle Road), the fastest route from Kona to Hilo, tops out at about 6,632 feet; the Maunakea Visitor Information Station is at about 9,200 feet and the summit 13,796 feet; Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park spans sea level to over 13,000 feet, and the Kīlauea summit area most visitors drive to sits several thousand feet up. Any of these after diving adds decompression stress the same way a flight does. Schedule the mountain and the volcano before your dive days, or allow at least the Divers Alert Network flying intervals afterward. Details are in our flying and altitude guide.

The state's recompression chamber is in Honolulu, so a serious diving injury on the Big Island means a medical flight to Oʻahu. That is one more reason to dive conservatively here and carry dive accident insurance.

Do the volcano on day one and the mantas on day two. Never the other way round.
