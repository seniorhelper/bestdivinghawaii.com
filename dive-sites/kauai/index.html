Kauaʻi Dive Sites

Lava tubes, caverns and turtles, with a north shore that only opens in summer. Every major Kauaʻi site with its depth, entry, season and the safety notes that matter.

Best Diving Hawaiʻi · Updated October 2026 · How we research

What is the best scuba diving on Kauaʻi?

Poʻipū on the south shore is Kauaʻi's year-round base: Koloa Landing (10–40 ft, easy shore entry) and Sheraton Caverns (30–60 ft, boat). In summer, Tunnels Beach on the north shore and the long charters to Niʻihau and Lehua open up. Kauaʻi has no MLCD, and nearly every dive entry is unguarded.

Kauaʻi is the oldest of the main islands, and its underwater landscape shows it: lava tubes, ledges, arches and caverns worn into the reef by long exposure to open-ocean swell. Coral cover is thinner than on Maui or the Kona coast, because so much swell reaches the island from every direction. What you get instead is structure, turtles in numbers and, on the summer trips to Niʻihau and Lehua, some of the clearest water in Hawaiʻi.

Kauaʻi also asks more of divers in terms of planning. The north shore is a summer-only coast. The south shore around Poʻipū carries most of the diving the rest of the year. And Kauaʻi has no Marine Life Conservation District at all, which makes good personal conduct on the reef matter even more.

North Shore, South Shore, and the Forbidden Island

  • South shore (Poʻipū and Kōloa). The year-round base. Koloa Landing is the island's most-used shore dive; Sheraton Caverns, Brennecke's Ledge and a string of other boat sites are a short ride offshore.
  • North shore (Hanalei to Hāʻena). Tunnels (Makua) Beach and its outer reef are among Hawaiʻi's best shore dives in summer and dangerous in winter.
  • Niʻihau and Lehua. Niʻihau is a privately owned island off Kauaʻi's west side; Lehua, the crescent islet beside it, is a state seabird sanctuary. Divers visit both only from boats, on long summer charters from Kauaʻi.

The east side around Līhuʻe and Nāwiliwili has boat sites too, but trade-wind chop makes it less dependable.

The Kauaʻi Seasons

Kauaʻi sits farther north and west than the other main islands and catches North Pacific swell first. From roughly October to April, north- and northwest-facing coasts take the big surf; the National Weather Service issues a high surf advisory for north-facing shores at 15 feet and a warning at 25. That is when Tunnels is off limits and the diving moves to Poʻipū, which is usually at its calmest in the same months.

In summer, the pattern swaps. South swells from the Southern Hemisphere bring surge to Poʻipū, and on those days the south-shore caverns can be uncomfortable or closed. The north shore usually flattens out, and Niʻihau charters start running when the open-ocean crossing is calm enough. Koloa Landing is the exception that proves the rule: local divers describe it as diveable almost year-round, with occasional summer closures when a south swell hits.

After heavy rain, rivers such as the Hanalei and Wailua turn the nearshore water brown. Kauaʻi's Ocean Safety Bureau issues brown-water advisories, and you should stay out until the water clears.

Koloa Landing: The Island's Training Ground

If you only shore dive once on Kauaʻi, it will probably be here. Koloa Landing sits at the mouth of a small inlet in Poʻipū, and the old boat ramp makes an easy entry. The reef runs about 10 to 40 feet with a maximum near 50, there are turtles on almost every dive, and at night the reef fills with octopus, eels and nudibranchs. The lot at the end of Hoʻonani Road is small; go early, and be considerate of the homes around it.

Tunnels in Summer: How to Do It Right

Tunnels Beach takes its name from the lava tubes in its outer reef, which drops from about 15 feet to 60. Getting there is the hard part. Parking near Hāʻena is scarce, and the neighboring Hāʻena State Park requires visitors to reserve entry or parking in advance, so many divers arrive at dawn or use a shop that runs guided trips. From the beach it is a long surface swim across the shallow lagoon to the drop-off, and currents run in the channels through the reef. Only dive it on a flat summer day, with a buddy and a float, and turn back early if you are tired.

Planning a Niʻihau Trip

A Niʻihau day is a serious undertaking: roughly two and a half hours of open ocean each way from Kauaʻi's west side, three dives at remote walls and arches, and no shelter if the wind comes up. Operators run these trips mostly in the summer window and cancel freely when swell or wind is forecast, so build spare days into your plan. Expect advanced-level profiles, current and big blue water. Hawaiian monk seals are sometimes seen, and they must be given space; NOAA asks for at least 50 feet on land and in the water.

Because the island is private, there is no landing on Niʻihau. Lehua's status as a seabird sanctuary also rules out going ashore there.

Rules When There Is No MLCD

None of Hawaiʻi's eleven Marine Life Conservation Districts is on Kauaʻi. That does not mean anything goes. Statewide fishing rules, size limits and closed seasons still apply; sea turtles, monk seals and spinner dolphins are protected by federal law; and the statewide ban on selling sunscreens with oxybenzone or octinoxate applies here as everywhere. Treat every Kauaʻi reef as you would a reserve: no touching, no collecting and no standing on coral. Our marine-life etiquette guide sets out the distances.

Lifeguards, Kōkeʻe and Getting Home

Kauaʻi's lifeguards are part of the county's Ocean Safety Bureau, which covers the north, east and south-west districts from towers at the busiest beaches. Kauaʻi officials say about 95 percent of the island's drownings happen at unguarded beaches, and nearly every dive entry on the island is unguarded. Check Hawaii Beach Safety and the NWS surf forecast before every shore dive.

The road up Waimea Canyon to Kōkeʻe State Park climbs to about 4,000 feet at the Kalalau Lookout. That is lower than Haleakalā or Saddle Road, but it is still an altitude gain after diving. Plan the canyon drive for a non-dive day or allow a generous surface interval. The nearest recompression chamber is in Honolulu, a flight away, and inter-island flights count as flying after diving; see our flying and altitude guide.

Kauaʻi rewards divers who follow the swell: Poʻipū in winter, Tunnels and Niʻihau in summer.
