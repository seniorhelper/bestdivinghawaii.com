Oʻahu Dive Sites

Wrecks off Honolulu, summer lava arches on the North Shore, turtles on the leeward coast. Here is every major Oʻahu site with its depth, entry, season and the rules that apply.

Best Diving Hawaiʻi · Updated October 2026 · How we research

What is the best scuba diving on Oʻahu?

Oʻahu is Hawaiʻi's best wreck-diving island. The YO-257 and San Pedro off Waikīkī (about 60–100 ft), the Sea Tiger and the Corsair plane wreck are short boat rides from Honolulu and dive in most months. In summer, Shark's Cove and Three Tables in the Pūpūkea MLCD are the best shore dives; in winter, big surf closes them. Most wrecks need Advanced Open Water.

Oʻahu is the wreck island. Within a short boat ride of Honolulu sit several purpose-sunk ships and a WWII-era fighter plane, all on open sand where visibility is usually good. Add a summer-only North Shore with lava arches and protected reef, a leeward west coast with turtles and warm water, and the snorkel-first bay at Hanauma, and you have the most varied diving of any island for the least driving.

What Oʻahu does not have is many easy deep shore dives in winter. The North Shore turns into a surf contest venue from roughly October to April, and the south shore's big wrecks sit below the 60-foot Open Water limit. Knowing those two facts before you book saves most of the disappointment visiting divers run into here.

How Oʻahu's Diving Is Laid Out

Think of the island as four coasts that rarely work on the same day.

  • South shore (Honolulu to Hawaiʻi Kai). The boat-diving hub. The YO-257, San Pedro and Sea Tiger lie off Waikīkī and Honolulu, the Corsair off Hawaiʻi Kai. Trips are short and run in most months because winter's North Pacific swell does not reach this coast.
  • West side (Kahe Point to Waiʻanae). Leeward, dry and warm. Electric Beach is the well-known shore dive; the Mahi wreck is the boat dive. Calm much of the year, but large winter northwest swells wrap down this coast.
  • North Shore (Haleʻiwa to Pūpūkea). Spectacular in summer, closed in winter. Shark's Cove and Three Tables sit inside the Pūpūkea Marine Life Conservation District.
  • Southeast (Hanauma Bay). A city-run nature preserve and Hawaiʻi's first MLCD. Superb snorkeling, but it is not a practical scuba site for most visitors.

The windward (east) side gets steady trade-wind chop and is rarely where visiting divers spend their time.

Reading Oʻahu's Swell Seasons

Surf in Hawaiʻi comes from distant storms, so it arrives in seasons and from predictable directions. University of Hawaiʻi surf climatology for Oʻahu describes large northwest swells in winter and smaller south swells, often 3 to 9 feet, arriving in summer from Southern Hemisphere storms. Trade winds blow from the northeast for most of the year and are strongest in winter and spring.

For divers that translates into a simple rule: winter favors the south shore, summer opens the North Shore, and the west side is good unless a big northwest swell is wrapping around Kaʻena Point. The National Weather Service office in Honolulu issues a high surf advisory for north-facing shores when surf is expected to reach 15 feet (face height) and a warning at 25 feet. South-facing shores get an advisory at 10 feet. Any advisory on the coast you planned to shore dive means pick another coast.

Brown water is the other Oʻahu variable. After heavy rain, streams and storm drains around Honolulu flush into the nearshore water; the state Department of Health posts brown-water advisories and visibility near shore can drop to almost nothing. Boat sites a mile out are often still fine.

Boat or Shore on Oʻahu?

For the wrecks there is no shore option; they are too far out and too deep. Most south-shore two-tank trips leave in the morning from Honolulu harbors, with Kewalo Basin the most common departure, and are back before lunch. Parking at the harbors is metered, so bring a card or a parking app and allow extra time.

Shore diving on Oʻahu means renting tanks from a shop and driving to the site. Electric Beach works from a beach-park lot with a sandy entry. Shark's Cove and Three Tables in summer have a small roadside lot that fills early on weekends, and the lava entry is sharp: hard-soled booties and gloves you will not use on the reef are a good idea. Leave nothing visible in the car at any beach lot.

What the North Shore "Shark Dive" Really Is

Many lists, including earlier versions of this page, describe a scuba shark dive off Haleʻiwa. That is not how these trips work. The North Shore shark tours run about three miles offshore into water more than 250 feet deep, where Galapagos and sandbar sharks gather. Guests stay at the surface, either in a cage or, with cage-free operators, snorkeling or free-diving with a guide. No scuba certification is required, and tank diving is not part of the trip.

If you want sharks on scuba, your realistic encounters on Oʻahu are whitetip reef sharks resting under ledges on the wrecks and in summer at Pūpūkea. They are small, calm and usually asleep.

Protected Areas and Rules on Oʻahu

Oʻahu has three Marine Life Conservation Districts that divers meet. Hanauma Bay, established in 1967, was the first in the state. Pūpūkea, established in 1983 and enlarged in 2003, covers about 100 acres including Shark's Cove, Three Tables and Waimea Bay. The Waikīkī MLCD is a small nearshore strip. In all of them, taking or harming fish, shells, coral, rock or other marine life is prohibited; our MLCD rules guide explains the details.

Wildlife rules apply everywhere, not just inside the districts. NOAA asks people to stay at least 10 feet from sea turtles and 50 feet from Hawaiian monk seals, which haul out on Oʻahu beaches more often than visitors expect. Under Hawaiʻi's boating rules (HAR §13-245-9), boats must stay at least 100 feet from a displayed diver's flag, and divers should not surface more than 100 feet from their flag, a rule that matters for shore divers towing a float. See marine-life etiquette for the full list.

Ocean Safety, Chamber and Flying

Lifeguards on Oʻahu are part of the City and County of Honolulu's Ocean Safety division, which covers close to 200 miles of coastline. Towers staff popular beaches, but most dive entries are not guarded. Check Hawaii Beach Safety for posted hazards and the NWS Honolulu surf forecast the evening before, then look at the water yourself before you commit.

Oʻahu is also home to the state's emergency recompression facility, the University of Hawaiʻi Hyperbaric Treatment Center at Kuakini Medical Center, which Divers Alert Network designates as its Pacific regional coordination center. In an emergency, call 911 first, give oxygen if you have it and tell responders about the dives. Dive accident insurance is worth carrying.

Oʻahu's roads stay low, so altitude is rarely an issue here, but an inter-island flight counts as flying after diving. Divers Alert Network suggests at least 12 hours after a single no-decompression dive and 18 hours after repetitive or multi-day diving. Our flying and altitude guide covers the neighbor-island volcano roads too.

A Sensible Oʻahu Dive Plan

For a week in winter, book south-shore boat dives for the wrecks, keep a west-side shore dive in reserve for a calm morning, and snorkel Hanauma on a weekday you have reserved. In summer, flip it: dive Shark's Cove and Three Tables early in the morning before the crowds, and use the wrecks on days when a south swell stirs up the inshore reefs. Either way, keep your last dive at least 18 hours before your flight home.

On Oʻahu the question is never whether the diving is good. It is which coast is good today.
