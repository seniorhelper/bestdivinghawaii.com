Hawaiʻi Dive Experiences

Mantas in the lights, open-ocean drifters in the dark, a sunken crater and sunlit lava tubes. What each signature dive asks of you, and where to do it.

Best Diving Hawaiʻi · Updated October 2026 · How we research

What are the best dive experiences in Hawaiʻi?

The Kona manta night dive (Open Water, year-round) tops the list. Add blackwater off Kona for experienced night divers, Oʻahu's wrecks (mostly Advanced), the Lānaʻi Cathedrals caverns, Molokini crater and, in summer, Niʻihau from Kauaʻi. North Shore shark tours are surface trips, not scuba.

Hawaiʻi does not have the coral density of Indonesia or the shark numbers of the Bahamas. What it has is a set of dives you cannot do anywhere else in the same way: resident reef mantas feeding in lights off Kona, larval open-ocean animals rising past you in the dark, a sunken volcanic crater, lava tubes lit by shafts of sun, and whale song carrying through the water every winter. This page sorts those experiences by what they ask of you and where they happen, so you can build a trip around the ones you care about.

Every experience here links to a fuller briefing. Where an experience is commonly misdescribed online, we say so.

Manta Night Dive, Kona

The signature Hawaiʻi dive. After dark, divers settle on sand at about 25 to 45 feet at Manta Village off Keauhou or Garden Eel Cove near the Kona airport, while lights draw plankton and resident reef mantas come in to feed. Snorkelers watch from lit boards at the surface on separate trips. It is within Open Water limits and runs most nights of the year, though wind, swell and the mantas themselves decide each evening. Read the manta dive briefing.

Blackwater Night Dive, Kona

A blackwater dive is the opposite of a reef dive. The boat goes offshore over very deep water after dark, and divers hang on tethers beneath it in the upper water column, with lights pointed outward. What drifts into the light are the animals of the open ocean that rise toward the surface at night: larval fish, squid, siphonophores and creatures most divers have never seen. There is no bottom and no visual reference beyond your light and the down line, so operators generally want Advanced certification and night-dive experience. Our night diving guide covers it in detail.

Wreck Diving, Oʻahu

Oʻahu's south shore has Hawaiʻi's best concentration of wrecks, all purpose-sunk or long settled on open sand: the YO-257 and the smaller San Pedro off Waikīkī, the Sea Tiger off Honolulu, the Corsair, a WWII-era fighter that went down in 1946 off east Oʻahu, and on the west side the Mahi, a former minesweeper. Most lie between about 70 and 115 feet, so this is Advanced-diver territory, and Nitrox is useful. The upside is reliability: the south shore is sheltered from winter's big North Pacific swell. See Oʻahu dive sites and our wreck diving article.

Caverns and Lava Tubes

Lava flowing into the sea leaves tubes, arches and overhangs, and Hawaiʻi's reefs are full of them. The best known are the Cathedrals off Lānaʻi, two cavern sites where light pours through openings in the ceiling, at roughly 15 to 60 feet. Sheraton Caverns off Poʻipū on Kauaʻi, the arches at Five Caves on Maui and Shark's Cove on Oʻahu's North Shore in summer are other favorites. These are caverns with daylight in view, not cave dives, but they are overhead environments where surge can be strong. Do them on calm days with good buoyancy.

Molokini Crater

A crescent of volcanic rim three miles off southwest Maui, protected as a Marine Life Conservation District. The inside of the crescent is a sheltered reef where Discover Scuba students and new divers spend their first dives, at roughly 20 to 70 feet. The outer Back Wall is a drift along a face that drops past 200 feet, for experienced divers only. Boats tie to state moorings, not anchors, and leave early to beat the trade winds. Details are on the Maui dive sites page.

Sharks: What Is Real and What Is Not

You will see sharks diving in Hawaiʻi, mostly whitetip reef sharks resting under ledges and in caves, on the Oʻahu wrecks and at sites such as Five Caves, Puakō and Molokini. They are small and calm. Galapagos and sandbar sharks gather offshore of Oʻahu's North Shore, and shark tours from Haleʻiwa take guests out about three miles into deep water to see them, either from a cage or snorkeling with a guide. Those tours are surface experiences, not scuba dives; earlier versions of this page described them as a scuba shark dive, which was wrong. Some Maui operators also run a seasonal hammerhead trip off Molokaʻi for advanced divers.

Turtles and Reef Shore Dives

Green sea turtles are the one animal you can count on in Hawaiʻi. They rest on wrecks, under ledges and in sand channels, and visit cleaning stations where reef fish pick their shells. Two Step and Puakō on the Big Island, Mala Wharf and Five Caves on Maui, Koloa Landing on Kauaʻi and Electric Beach on Oʻahu are all reliable. NOAA's guideline is to stay at least 10 feet away in and out of the water and never block a turtle's route to the surface. Our shore diving guide covers entries and rules.

Remote Big-Water Days: Niʻihau and Lehua

From Kauaʻi's west side, full-day charters cross roughly two and a half hours of open ocean to dive the walls and arches of Niʻihau and Lehua. The water is often the clearest in the state, and monk seals and pelagic sharks are possibilities. Trips run mostly in summer and cancel when swell or wind is up. Bring Advanced experience, seasickness prevention and a flexible schedule.

Whale Season

From about November to May, humpback whales are in Hawaiian waters, with the greatest numbers in the middle of that window. On many winter dives, especially around Maui, you will hear males singing, a sound that seems to come from everywhere at once. You will rarely see whales underwater, and you must not try to: federal rules prohibit approaching within 100 yards by any means. Enjoy the soundtrack and leave the whale-watching to the boat ride. See marine-life etiquette.

What Each Experience Asks of You

Certification cards tell a shop what you were trained for. They do not say how a particular dive will feel. Before you book, think about the specific demands:

  • Darkness. The manta and blackwater dives happen at night. The manta dive is shallow and stationary; blackwater is mid-water with no bottom. If you have never dived at night, do a reef night dive or a dusk dive first.
  • Depth and gas. Oʻahu's wrecks and Molokini's Back Wall reach depths where no-decompression time runs out quickly. Good air consumption and a dive computer you understand matter more here than anywhere else on the list.
  • Current. The Back Wall is dived as a drift, and Niʻihau's walls can run. Practice deploying a surface marker buoy before the trip.
  • Overheads. The Cathedrals and Sheraton Caverns put rock above you. They are not technical dives, but the surge inside lava tubes on swell days catches people out.
  • Boat time. Niʻihau means about five hours of crossings in a day; Molokini and Lānaʻi crossings can be choppy by late morning. Seasickness can end a trip before the first dive.
  • Surface swims. Shore experiences such as Tunnels and Two Step involve swimming on the surface in full gear, sometimes against wind chop.

Sample Experience-Led Weeks

A winter week (December to March)

Fly into Kona. Spend the first day on the volcano or Maunakea, while you are not yet diving. Then dive the Kona coast for three days, with the manta dive on the first night in case a later night is cancelled. Take a dive-free morning and fly to Maui or Oʻahu: on Maui, Molokini and a south-shore shore dive, with whale song on most dives; on Oʻahu, the south-shore wrecks, which are sheltered from winter swell. Leave a full day free before flying home.

A summer week (June to September)

Start on Kauaʻi for Koloa Landing, Sheraton Caverns and, if the weather window holds, a Niʻihau charter. Fly to Oʻahu on a non-dive morning and dive Shark's Cove and Three Tables early in the day while the North Shore is flat, then add a wreck dive. As in winter, keep the last day dry.

These are outlines, not bookings. Swap islands to taste, but keep the two principles: put altitude before diving, and put the dive you care about most early enough to have a backup day.

How to Choose

If you have one night on the Big Island

Do the manta dive, at whichever site the operator recommends for the conditions. If you are an experienced night diver with a second night, consider blackwater.

If you are newly certified

Molokini's inside reef, the manta dive, Mala Wharf, Ulua Beach and Koloa Landing are all within your training. Save the Oʻahu wrecks, Molokini's Back Wall and Niʻihau for when you have Advanced certification and more dives logged.

If you want structure and history

Spend your days on Oʻahu's south shore wrecks, then add the Lānaʻi Cathedrals on a Maui leg.

If you want the calmest water

Base yourself on the Kona coast, which the volcanoes shelter from the trades and most winter swell.

Gear Notes for Hawaiʻi's Signature Dives

Shops on every island rent complete kits, so you can travel light. A few items are worth bringing or requesting. A well-fitting mask of your own removes the most common source of discomfort. A surface marker buoy and a whistle or other signaling device matter on drift dives and boat dives in blue water, where a lone head is hard to spot. For night dives, a primary light and a backup. For warmth, a 3 mm full suit covers most daytime diving, but the manta and blackwater dives, where you stay still in the dark, are where a 5 mm suit or a hood earns its place. And for every sunny surface interval, mineral sunscreen and a hat.

Booking Well

  • Book the experience you care about first, early in your stay, so a cancellation leaves you spare days.
  • Be honest about your experience. Shops place divers on boats and sites by logged dives as well as certification.
  • Ask about group size and guide ratio, what is included, and the plan if the primary site is blown out.
  • Check the cancellation policy, especially for weather-dependent trips such as Niʻihau, Molokini's Back Wall and the manta dive.
  • Plan altitude around diving. Haleakalā, Saddle Road and Maunakea come before your dive days, not after; see our flying and altitude guide.
