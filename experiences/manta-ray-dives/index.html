Kona Manta Ray Night Dive

Reef mantas feeding in the lights off Kona, a dive Open Water divers can do. Which site, what to bring, how to behave and what can cancel the night.

Best Diving Hawaiʻi · Updated October 2026 · How we research

What is the Kona manta ray night dive?

After dark off Kona, divers settle on sand at about 25–45 ft at Manta Village or Garden Eel Cove while lights draw plankton and resident reef mantas come in to feed. Open Water certification is enough; snorkelers watch from lit boards at the surface. It runs year-round, weather permitting. Never touch the mantas.

The Kona manta night dive is the one dive almost every visiting diver in Hawaiʻi wants to do, and it is one of the few famous dives that is genuinely suitable for newly certified divers. You descend after dark to a sandy patch about 30 to 40 feet down, settle in around a cluster of lights, and wait. The lights draw plankton. The plankton draws reef manta rays. For the next 45 minutes or so, if the mantas show, they loop and somersault through the beams just over your head.

This page is the practical briefing: which site, diver or snorkeler, what training and gear you need, how to behave around the animals and what can cancel the night. For the longer story of the mantas themselves, sighting odds and current prices, read our Kona manta night dive guide.

If you are newer to night diving or prone to seasickness, ask which site the operator expects to use and whether they move to the calmer one when the wind or swell is up. If you have a choice of departure harbor, Keauhou is closer to Manta Village and Honokōhau to Garden Eel Cove.

Diver or Snorkeler?

Divers settle on the sand in a loose ring around a light source the crew places on the bottom, or around a central "campfire" of lights. You stay low and still, keep your own light pointed up into the water column, and let the mantas come down to feed. The view is upward, with the mantas silhouetted against the lit water and the surface.

Snorkelers hold onto a floating light board at the surface and look down. The mantas feed in the same column of light, so they often pass within a few feet of the board. You need to be comfortable floating face-down in open water at night, usually in a wetsuit and with a flotation aid the operator provides. Operators generally run snorkelers and divers on separate dedicated trips.

A mixed group where some people dive and some snorkel can usually be accommodated by booking the same operator's two trips for the same night, but ask. Non-swimmers sometimes ride along to watch from the deck.

Training and Experience

An Open Water certification is enough for the manta dive at either site; depths stay within the 60-foot Open Water range. What matters more than the card is comfort. You will be on a boat at dusk, descending into dark water and holding still for most of the dive. If you have never dived at night, many operators run the trip as a two-tank outing, with a late-afternoon reef dive followed by the manta dive after dark, which gives you a chance to settle your weighting and buoyancy while it is still light. A night-dive orientation or refresher in the days before helps too; see our night diving guide.

Good buoyancy is the main skill. Kneeling on the sand is common, but you should be able to stay put without flailing and without rising off the bottom when a manta passes close.

What to Bring

  • Warmth. You will be still for most of the dive, at night. Even in 78 °F water many divers want a 5 mm suit or a hood. Snorkelers lose heat at the surface too.
  • A dive light, plus a backup. Operators usually provide them; bring your own if you prefer.
  • Seasickness prevention if you need it, taken in time. The boat waits on a mooring while the light fades.
  • A warm layer for the ride back, and a towel. Kona evenings on the water get cool when you are wet.
  • Mineral sunscreen for the daytime part of the trip; Hawaiʻi County allows only mineral sunscreens to be sold. See our sunscreen rules.

How to Behave Around Mantas

The rules are short, and the best operators brief them every night:

  1. Never touch a manta. Their skin carries a protective mucous layer, and contact can damage it.
  2. Stay low and still. Do not rise up into the water column to meet them, swim after them or block their path.
  3. Point your light up, not at the mantas' eyes and not across the sand into other divers' faces. The beam is there to gather plankton.
  4. Keep fins and hands tucked in so a manta that misjudges a loop does not collide with you.
  5. Follow the crew's lighting and positioning plan, especially when several boats share a site.

Hawaiʻi protects manta rays in state law. Act 92, signed in 2009, makes it an offense to knowingly catch or kill a manta ray in state waters, with fines rising to $10,000 for repeat offenses. There is no separate state rulebook for manta tours; the Manta Pacific Research Foundation has pushed since 2009 for standards for manta tourism. The etiquette above is what responsible operators already follow.

The Science Behind the Show

Kona's mantas are reef mantas, not the larger oceanic species, and they are residents. Researchers identify individuals by the unique pattern of spots on their bellies and have tracked many of the same animals for years. A 2023 genomic study of Hawaiian reef mantas found small, island-resident populations, which helps explain why the same mantas return to the same Kona sites night after night, and why disturbing them matters.

The feeding behavior is a simple chain. Light draws plankton up and into the beam. Mantas filter-feed on plankton, so where the plankton is densest the mantas feed, often doing slow backward loops to stay in the column. More plankton generally means more feeding.

How a Typical Manta Evening Runs

Exact times shift with the season and the operator, but the shape of the evening is consistent. You check in at the harbor in the late afternoon, sign waivers, show your certification card and get fitted for rental gear. The boat leaves while it is still light. On two-tank trips the first dive is a reef dive near the manta site around sunset, which is a good moment to fine-tune your weighting. During the surface interval the crew briefs the manta dive: where the lights will be, where divers kneel or hover, where snorkelers float, how to signal and how long you will stay down.

Once it is dark, divers descend together to the light area and settle in. The first manta can appear within minutes or not for a long while. When the dive ends, you ascend as a group along the mooring line or with your guide, make a safety stop, and climb back aboard in the dark, which is the moment to move slowly and follow the crew. The ride back to the harbor is short. Expect to be back on land later in the evening, hungry and cold, with a head full of mantas.

Photographing the Mantas

Most of the best manta images are taken from the bottom, looking up, with a wide-angle lens and the mantas lit by the dive lights already in the water. A small video light or the camera's own light is usually enough, and it helps to set exposure for the lit water rather than the black background. A few rules keep you and the animals safe:

  • Stay in your spot. Do not swim up or across the light area to get a better angle.
  • Keep the camera close to your body so a looping manta does not hit it.
  • Ask your operator about strobes before the dive; some prefer continuous lights only.
  • Put the camera down for part of the dive. It is easy to watch the whole encounter through a screen.

What Can Cancel the Night

Kona is sheltered from the trade winds, which is why the manta dive runs on most nights of the year. It is not immune. Large west or south swells, southerly kona storms and strong wind can make the sites unsafe or push the operator to the more sheltered site. Mantas also simply do not show some nights. Many operators offer a credit or a repeat trip when no mantas appear; check the policy when you book. If the manta dive is the reason for your trip, schedule it early in your stay so you have spare nights.

Both manta sites are boat dives, so your crew is your first line of safety; there are no lifeguards offshore. On Hawaiʻi Island, shore lifeguards belong to the Hawaiʻi Fire Department's Ocean Safety section, and the National Weather Service in Honolulu sets a lower high-surf threshold for the Big Island's west-facing shores than for other islands: an advisory at 8 feet. If one is posted for your evening, expect a site change or a cancellation.

After the Dive: Mind the Mountain

A manta dive followed by a drive across Saddle Road to Hilo, or a sunrise trip up Maunakea the next morning, puts you at altitude within hours of diving. Saddle Road tops out around 6,632 feet and the Maunakea visitor station sits at about 9,200 feet. Plan the mountain before the mantas, not after; our flying and altitude guide has the intervals.

Settle in, light up, keep still. The mantas do the rest.
