What's the best diving on Oahu, and who do I book with?

Oahu is Hawaii's wreck-diving capital. The signature site is the Sea Tiger, a 150-plus-foot ship and one of Hawaii's deepest popular recreational wrecks, with its deck around 60 to 90 feet. The YO-257 and neighboring San Pedro are paired artificial-reef wrecks full of turtles and reef sharks, and the Corsair is a WWII fighter plane resting deeper for advanced divers. Most Oahu dive shops launch from Kewalo Basin, just 10 to 15 minutes from Waikiki, run small groups, and offer reef dives, wreck dives, and full certification courses.

Diving Oahu

Why Divers Love Oahu

Oahu's diving has a personality all its own. While other islands lead with reefs and big animals, Oahu is about wrecks — a south-shore graveyard of ships and a sunken warplane, most of them placed as artificial reefs and now draped in coral and crowded with life. For wreck divers and history buffs, no Hawaiian island compares.

The convenience is unbeatable, too. Boats leave from Kewalo Basin just minutes from Waikiki, so you can dive a 150-foot ship in the morning and be back at the beach by lunch. The wrecks suit a range of levels, from accessible multi-level dives to deep advanced profiles, and the reefs between them brim with turtles and rays.

This guide covers the best Oahu dive sites and the operators who run them. For the island's full site list, see our Oahu dive sites guide, and compare operators statewide on our main Hawaii dive shops hub.

The Sites

The Best Oahu Dive Sites

Most Oahu diving happens along the south shore, a short boat ride from Kewalo Basin with Diamond Head and the Honolulu skyline as your backdrop. Operators usually pair a deep wreck with a shallower reef on a two-tank trip.

The Sea Tiger

The Sea Tiger is Oahu's flagship wreck and one of Hawaii's deepest popular recreational dives. A former Chinese trading vessel over 150 feet long, it was sunk in 1999 and now sits remarkably intact. The deck and superstructure run roughly 60 to 90 feet, with the sand bottom near 100 to 120 feet, so divers enjoy a good multi-level profile. Swim-throughs and penetration points abound, and the wreck teems with green sea turtles, whitetip reef sharks, eagle rays, moray eels, and schooling fish.

The YO-257 & San Pedro

These two wrecks sit close together and are usually dived as a pair. The YO-257, a 175-foot former Navy yard oiler sunk in 1989 as an artificial reef, rests upright around 97 to 100 feet, with its deck near 70 to 90 feet and abundant swim-throughs. A short swim away, the San Pedro lies a touch shallower. Both are turtle magnets — the San Pedro's deck even serves as a turtle cleaning station — and whitetip reef sharks often shelter in the structure.

The Corsair & South Shore Reefs

The Corsair is the one Oʻahu wreck that was not sunk on purpose: a WWII-era F4U fighter that went down in 1946 and now rests around 105 to 115 feet. Because it sits deep with no upper structure, bottom time is short, making it an advanced dive — but eagle rays, octopus, and nudibranchs reward those who go. Between wrecks, operators dive vibrant reefs like Fantasy Reef and the lava-formed Ewa Pinnacles, rich with reef fish and turtles.

Plan Your Dive

Best Time to Dive Oahu & What to Know

Oahu's south shore dives well year-round, but a little planning makes your trip smoother.

Season & Conditions

The south shore, where the wrecks lie, is sheltered from the big North Pacific swells of winter, which is why the wrecks dive well from about October to April. In summer, south swells from Southern Hemisphere storms can add surge and stir up the shallower reefs, though the deeper wrecks often stay diveable. North Shore sites, by contrast, are a summer-only proposition because of big winter surf. For wreck diving off Waikiki, almost any month works.

Certification & Depth

Match the wreck to your training. The Sea Tiger's deck and the YO-257 offer multi-level profiles many certified divers can enjoy, while the Corsair and deeper penetration dives call for advanced or wreck certifications. If you want to go deeper or inside the wrecks, ask about specialty courses — several Oahu shops offer them. Honest about your experience, and your guide will place you right.

Booking & Gear

Book ahead in summer and around holidays. Most operators include tanks and weights; confirm whether full gear rental is extra. Arrive about 30 minutes before departure for check-in, gear fitting, and the briefing. For month-by-month planning across the islands, see our best time to dive Hawaii guide, and read up on the animals in our marine life guide.

Getting to the Boat

Oʻahu Departure Harbors, Explained

Where your boat leaves from decides how long your morning is and which sites are realistic. Oʻahu's dive fleet works out of a handful of harbors, and each suits a different part of the coast.

Kewalo Basin (Honolulu)

The busiest dive harbor on the island, between downtown Honolulu and Waikīkī. It is home to part of Honolulu's commercial fishing fleet and to many of the charter and excursion boats that serve visitors. From here the YO-257, San Pedro and Sea Tiger are a short run. Parking is metered, so allow time to find a space and pay, and keep valuables out of sight.

Ala Wai Small Boat Harbor (Waikīkī)

At the western edge of Waikīkī, close enough to many hotels to walk with a small bag. Several shops run reef and wreck trips from here on the same south-shore sites.

Hawaiʻi Kai and the southeast

Boats on the east side of Honolulu reach the Corsair and the reefs and lava formations toward Koko Head. Check exactly which marina or ramp your operator uses; addresses differ between shops.

Waiʻanae coast (west side)

West-side trips head for the Mahi wreck and the leeward reefs. It is a long drive from Waikīkī, often 45 minutes or more in traffic, and big winter northwest swells can wrap down this coast.

Haleʻiwa (North Shore)

The North Shore shark tours leave from Haleʻiwa. Those are surface cage or snorkel trips about three miles offshore, not scuba dives. In summer some shops also run guided shore dives at Shark's Cove and Three Tables in the Pūpūkea MLCD.

Whichever harbor you use, aim to arrive at least half an hour before departure for check-in, waivers and gear fitting, and bring your certification card and logbook. If your last dive is the day before an inter-island or outbound flight, ask the shop to plan an early return; Divers Alert Network suggests at least 18 hours between repetitive diving and flying.
