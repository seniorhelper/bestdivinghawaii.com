Hawaiʻi Dive Shops

Dive operators on Oʻahu, Maui, the Big Island and Kauaʻi, and the practical knowledge to judge them: boat licensing, harbors, permits and the questions worth asking.

How do I find the right dive shop in Hawaiʻi?

Choose the island and coast first, then the operator. Ask each shop which site they plan and what the backup is, the guide-to-diver ratio, required experience, what is included, emergency oxygen on board and the cancellation policy. Boats with more than six passengers must be Coast Guard inspected. We list operators but do not rank them.

In Hawaiʻi the operator often matters more than the site. The same Molokini morning can be calm and unhurried on one boat and a crowded scramble on another, and the shop decides whether you dive the plan or the backup when the swell comes up. This page lists dive operators by island and, more usefully, explains how Hawaiʻi's dive businesses work so you can judge them yourself.

How Hawaiʻi Dive Operations Work

Boat size and licensing

Many Hawaiʻi dive boats are small. Federal rules draw a line at six paying passengers: a captain can carry up to six with a U.S. Coast Guard operator license on an uninspected vessel (the "six-pack" license), while boats carrying more than six passengers for hire must be Coast Guard inspected and carry a Certificate of Inspection. Neither is automatically better. Small boats mean small groups and flexible plans; larger inspected vessels give a steadier ride on choppy channel crossings. It is reasonable to ask which kind of boat you will be on.

Permits for protected areas

At Molokini, commercial dive, snorkel and paddle operations need a state permit, and boats use state day-use moorings with a time limit rather than anchoring. That is why Molokini departures are early and schedules are firm. Our MLCD rules guide explains the background.

Departure points

  • Oʻahu: Kewalo Basin and Ala Wai harbor in Honolulu for the south-shore wrecks; Hawaiʻi Kai for the Corsair side; the Waiʻanae coast for the west.
  • Maui: Māʻalaea Harbor and the Kīhei Boat Ramp for Molokini; West Maui for Lānaʻi. Lahaina Small Boat Harbor reopened to limited daytime commercial use in December 2025 after the 2023 fire.
  • Hawaiʻi Island: Honokōhau Harbor north of Kailua-Kona and Keauhou Bay to the south.
  • Kauaʻi: the Poʻipū area for south-shore diving; the west side for Niʻihau charters.

Guided shore diving

On every island, shops run guided shore dives that meet at the beach. They are a good way to learn local entries before diving on your own; see our shore diving guide.

What to Ask Before You Pay a Deposit

  1. Which site, and what is the backup? In Hawaiʻi the answer to "where are we diving?" is often "it depends on the swell." A good shop has a clear plan B on a sheltered coast.
  2. How many divers per guide? And will certified divers and Discover Scuba students be split?
  3. What training and experience do you require for this trip? Expect logbook questions for the Oʻahu wrecks, Molokini's Back Wall, Niʻihau and night dives.
  4. What is included? Tanks and weights, full rental, Nitrox, snacks, hotel pickup.
  5. What is on board for emergencies? Emergency oxygen, a first-aid kit and a radio are basic expectations for any dive boat.
  6. What happens if the trip is cancelled, or if no mantas show on a manta trip?
  7. Where exactly do we meet, and where do I park?

Good operators answer these readily. If the answers are vague, keep looking.

Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

  • No questions about your experience. A shop that books you onto a deep wreck or a drift without asking about your certification, logged dives or last dive is not matching sites to divers.
  • "We always go, whatever the weather." In Hawaiʻi, good operators cancel or move when swell or wind makes a site unsafe. Being willing to cancel is a sign of judgment, not weakness.
  • Touching or feeding wildlife. Guides who handle turtles, chase dolphins or feed fish are breaking rules or guidelines and teaching guests to do the same.
  • Vague answers about the boat. You should be able to find out who the captain is, what kind of vessel it is and what emergency equipment is aboard.

Bring These With You

  • Your certification card (or app) and logbook.
  • Dive accident insurance details. Hawaiʻi's recompression facility is in Honolulu, so injuries on other islands involve a medical flight.
  • A completed medical questionnaire if the shop sends one ahead, and a physician's sign-off if any answer requires it.
  • Mineral sunscreen; Maui County allows only mineral sunscreens to be sold or used. See our sunscreen rules.
  • Your flight times, so the shop can keep your last dive at least 18 hours before you fly. See our flying and altitude guide.
